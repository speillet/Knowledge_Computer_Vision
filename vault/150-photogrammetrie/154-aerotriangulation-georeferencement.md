## CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-001

Objective: Judge the accuracy of an adjusted block from independent checkpoints rather than from control residuals. Family: evaluation. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-001
Question: A contractor delivers a drone block adjusted with four 3D ground control points (GCPs). The report lists GCP height residuals of +0.8, −0.6, +0.5 and −0.7 cm. Six surveyed points kept out of the adjustment give errors, product minus survey, in cm: $dx=(3,-2,4,-1,2,-3)$, $dy=(-2,3,1,-4,2,1)$, $dz=(6,8,-2,7,9,5)$. The checkpoint survey has an RMSE of 1 cm horizontally and 2 cm vertically; the contract asks for $\mathrm{RMSE}_H\le5$ cm and $\mathrm{RMSE}_V\le10$ cm under the ASPRS positional accuracy standards, Edition 2. (a) Compute the horizontal and vertical accuracy as that edition defines it. (b) What do the GCP residuals tell you about accuracy, and why? (c) Can the delivery be accepted as compliant, and what do you ask for?
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) $\mathrm{RMSE}_X=\sqrt{43/6}=2.68$, $\mathrm{RMSE}_Y=\sqrt{35/6}=2.42$, so $\mathrm{RMSE}_{H1}=\sqrt{13}=3.61$ cm; $\mathrm{RMSE}_{V1}=\sqrt{259/6}=6.57$ cm. Edition 2 adds the checkpoint survey error: $\mathrm{RMSE}_H=\sqrt{3.61^2+1^2}=3.74$ cm and $\mathrm{RMSE}_V=\sqrt{6.57^2+2^2}=6.87$ cm, both below the targets on RMSE alone. (2) Almost nothing: they are fitted residuals of points the adjustment was pulled onto (RMS 0.66 cm), so they are shrunk by the redundancy of those observations, and any deformation of the block between control points is invisible at them; only points excluded from processing measure the product. (3) Not as compliant: the mean vertical error is 5.5 cm, above 25% of the 10 cm target, so a vertical bias must be investigated and explained before acceptance; and six checkpoints are fewer than the 30 required, so only the "tested using ONLY 6 checkpoints" statement is allowed. Ask for at least 30 independent, well-distributed checkpoints and the cause of the bias.

**Further explanation**: For a weighted least-squares adjustment with a correct model, the expected squared residual of an observation is its variance times its redundancy number, and the redundancy numbers sum to the number of observations minus the number of unknowns. Constructed case: one control coordinate surveyed at 1 cm and determined by the images at 3 cm, the rest of the block fixed, has redundancy $1/(1+9)=0.1$; its residual has a standard deviation of 0.32 cm, while the same point used as a checkpoint would show 3.16 cm, ten times more, without any systematic error. Real blocks add deformations that control pins down: James and Robson cite a survey in which the height error at check points had a standard deviation seven times that at control points.

**Reading the bias**: here the mean (5.5 cm) is most of the RMSE (6.57 cm; standard deviation 3.94 cm), so the error is mainly systematic. ASPRS assumes such biases have been removed and asks for the mean, median, standard deviation and error vectors to find them. Candidates include a height-system mix, a vertical lever arm, a principal-distance error or a dome; plot $dz$ against position. No checkpoint is a blunder (threshold three times the target, 30 cm), so none may be dropped. With six points, an RMSE also carries roughly 28% sampling uncertainty. Constructed numbers, recomputed by the check script of this subject.

**Blocking errors**: Reporting GCP residuals as product accuracy. Moving checkpoints into the adjustment to "improve" them. Averaging $\mathrm{RMSE}_X$ and $\mathrm{RMSE}_Y$ instead of adding their squares, or omitting the survey component. Accepting because the RMSE passes while the mean error flags a bias. Calling six checkpoints a compliant test.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/154-aerotriangulation-georeferencement|Full reference]]; [ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, Ed. 2 V2 (2024), §7.2, §7.12.1–§7.12.4, §7.13–§7.14, §7.16.1](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/251007_gt_cnig_quadogeo_251007_2024_asprs_positional_accuracy_standards_edition2_version2.pdf); [Triggs et al. 2000, §10.1 and §10.4](https://hal.science/inria-00548290/document); [James and Robson 2014, Discussion](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1443475/1/esp3609.pdf). Checkpoint values and the redundancy case are project constructions.

## CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-002

Objective: Build a direct-georeferencing ground-error budget and predict the signature of each term. Family: reasoning. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-002
Question: A survey aircraft flies at 2,000 m above flat ground at 70 m/s, with flight lines in alternating directions, and maps by direct georeferencing: GNSS/IMU exterior orientations held fixed, no ground control. After system calibration, the residual boresight error is 0.003° in roll and in pitch, the timing error between exposure and trajectory is up to 2 ms, the lever arm is known to 3 cm and the GNSS/IMU positions to 5 cm. (a) Estimate the ground displacement each term causes for a near-nadir point and combine them. (b) How would each term show up in checkpoints and in the overlap between lines flown in opposite directions, and at a different flying height? (c) Which of these errors can no comparison of flight lines reveal?
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) Boresight: $H\tan\delta\theta=2000\times5.24\cdot10^{-5}=0.105$ m per axis, across track for roll and along track for pitch; timing: $v\,\delta t=70\times0.002=0.14$ m along track; lever arm 0.03 m; GNSS 0.05 m; root-sum-square about 0.21 m if independent, pitch and timing alone giving 0.175 m along track. The two system-calibration terms dominate, not the GNSS. (2) Roll and pitch boresight errors and the horizontal lever-arm error are fixed in the aircraft body frame, and a timing error acts along the velocity, so all flip sign with flight direction: overlapping opposite lines disagree by twice the term (0.21 m for boresight, 0.28 m for timing). Pitch boresight and timing both act along track; the boresight term scales with flying height (0.052 m at 1,000 m) and the timing term with speed, so another height or speed separates them. (3) Constant offsets: the vertical lever-arm component, a GNSS height shift or a principal-distance error move every line the same way; only ground control or checkpoints reveal them.

**Further explanation**: Direct georeferencing has no redundancy in absolute orientation: the control is measured at the sensor and transferred to the ground, an extrapolation in which errors are not compensated as they are between ground control points. The boresight term grows with flying altitude, as the ASPRS addendum on direct georeferencing stresses; Cramer et al. report that misalignment angles differing by about $1.5\cdot10^{-3}$ degrees shift object points by about 5 cm from 2,000 m, and the same formula gives 0.052 m.

**Signatures and their limits**: A heading error rotates the footprint about nadir: 0.006° moves a point 1,000 m from nadir by 0.105 m and a nadir point not at all. An unresolved mid-exposure pulse may appear as a horizontal shift in the camera-station residuals after adjusting for flight direction. Heipke et al. note analogous equivalences: a principal-point shift along x mimics a timing error, and a focal-length change mimics a GNSS height shift unless two flying heights are flown. The root-sum-square treats the terms as independent random errors, but boresight and timing are systematic per line and do not average out over checkpoints; they shift means and create strip discrepancies. Constructed numbers, recomputed by the check script; the general delay-versus-translation argument is in CV-MULTICALIB.

**Blocking errors**: Treating an angular error as independent of flying height. Budgeting only the GNSS accuracy. Expecting many images to average a boresight or timing error away. Calling 2 ms negligible at aircraft speed. Assuming lines in alternating directions cancel these errors in the product.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/154-aerotriangulation-georeferencement|Full reference]]; [Cramer et al. 2000, §2.2 and §3.4.1](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIII/congress/part3/198_XXXIII-part3.pdf); [Heipke et al. 2001, §1 and §4](https://phowo.ifp.uni-stuttgart.de/publications/phowo01/Heipke.pdf); [ASPRS Ed. 2 V2 (2024), Addendum III §K and Addendum V §A.4.2](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/251007_gt_cnig_quadogeo_251007_2024_asprs_positional_accuracy_standards_edition2_version2.pdf). Error budget and sign analysis: project derivations.

## CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-003

Objective: Diagnose a radially symmetric height error in a self-calibrated nadir block and choose its mitigation. Family: diagnosis. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-003
Question: A 400 m × 400 m drone block was flown in parallel nadir lines at constant height over nearly flat ground, with a consumer camera whose lens parameters were self-calibrated in the bundle adjustment. Eight 3D GCPs lie on the perimeter, about 200 m from the block centre, with residuals of ±1 cm. Twelve independent checkpoints show no east–west or north–south trend, but against their distance $r$ from the block centre their height errors in cm are: at 20 m, 16 and 15; at 70 m, 14, 14 and 13; at 120 m, 10, 11, 10 and 9; at 170 m, 4, 5 and 4. Describe the error pattern, explain what produces it, say how you would confirm the cause, and state what you would change in acquisition or processing.
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) Pattern: a radially symmetric height deformation, largest at the centre and near zero at the perimeter control. It fits $dz\approx15.6-3.9\cdot10^{-4}r^2$ cm ($R^2=0.98$), which vanishes near $r=200$ m where the GCPs are; the mean (10.4 cm) is close to the RMSE (11.2 cm). It is systematic, and neither a datum shift (uniform) nor a tilt (linear in position). (2) Cause: doming. In a network of near-parallel nadir images, the self-calibrated radial distortion is correlated with the curvature of the reconstructed surface and inseparable from it without additional information, so measurement noise drives the radial term to a wrong value; perimeter control pins the edges and leaves the centre free. (3) Confirmation and remedy: rerun with a camera model fixed from an independent calibration, or with several fixed radial-term values to find the one that removes the radial trend, and inspect parameter correlations; then add oblique or convergent images, or lines at another height, and interior control and checkpoints at several radii.

**Further explanation**: James and Robson simulated self-calibrated parallel-axis blocks and found that, without control, camera angles varying by a standard deviation of 2° still left a dome of about 0.2 m over about 100 m. Adding a second set of nadir lines at a different heading did not significantly reduce it, while oblique images (about 20° in banked turns for fixed wings, 30° with a tiltable camera) reduced it by one to two orders of magnitude in their simulations. Control inside the adjustment reduces the dome but leaves part of it, depending on the relative weights of image and control measurements. Przybilla et al. also observed dome-shaped errors with GCPs only on a flat moorland, improved by adding RTK camera positions and one GCP.

**Mechanism in one stereo pair**: with fixed, correct orientations, a residual radial term $\delta k$ changes the x-parallax by a factor $1+\delta k\,q$, where $q$ is a quadratic form of the normalized image coordinates, so the depth error is a paraboloid centred on the model. Constructed case at 100 m depth with a 30 m base: 2.25 cm at the model centre, 8.99 cm under a camera and 11.24 cm 30 m across track. The fit and the stereo case are constructed and recomputed by the check script.

**Blocking errors**: Shifting or tilting the surface model to remove the error. Adding only perimeter GCPs. Trusting the low GCP residuals. Freeing more distortion coefficients. Expecting extra nadir lines at another heading to fix it.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/154-aerotriangulation-georeferencement|Full reference]]; [James and Robson 2014, Results (idealized parallel geometries, sensitivity to variability, practical flight plans, optimized processing), Discussion and Conclusions](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1443475/1/esp3609.pdf); [Przybilla et al. 2020, §3.2.1](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLIII-B1-2020/485/2020/isprs-archives-XLIII-B1-2020-485-2020.pdf). Checkpoint values, the radial fit and the stereo-pair derivation are project constructions.

## CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-004

Objective: Design the ground control and checkpoint plan of a drone block, with and without onboard RTK positions. Family: design. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-004
Question: You plan a drone survey of a 600 m × 400 m quarry at 2 cm ground sample distance, for an orthophoto and a surface model with targets $\mathrm{RMSE}_H\le5$ cm and $\mathrm{RMSE}_V\le5$ cm under the ASPRS standards, Edition 2. Your RTK rover surveys ground points with about 2.5 cm vertical RMSE. Specify (a) the accuracy required of GCPs and checkpoints, and the largest fit to checkpoints the product may show; (b) the GCP layout if the drone logs no usable camera positions, and how it changes if RTK or PPK camera positions are logged; (c) the number and placement of checkpoints.
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) GCPs and checkpoints must be at least twice as accurate as the product, at most 2.5 cm here, so the rover is at the limit. Because Edition 2 adds the checkpoint survey error, the fit to checkpoints may not exceed $\sqrt{5^2-2.5^2}=4.33$ cm: a fit of 4.5 cm fails at 5.15 cm, and a 1.5 cm survey would leave 4.77 cm. (2) Without camera positions: 3D GCPs around the perimeter, at the corners and along the edges, set at or slightly beyond the boundary so that nothing is extrapolated, plus interior control near the centre and at mid-radius, because a self-calibrated nadir block bends in height; fly opposite directions and add oblique images or a second height. With RTK or PPK positions weighted at their real accuracy: far fewer GCPs, but at least one near the centre and preferably a few at the corners, because a constant height offset and the principal distance–height correlation are not resolved by camera positions alone. (3) At least 30 checkpoints for a compliant statement, never used in the adjustment, away from GCPs, spread over the interior and the periphery at several distances from the centre, vertical ones on open, flat ground away from breaks.

**Further explanation**: ASPRS asks the aerial triangulation itself to reach half the product RMSE at checkpoints and places 3D control on the perimeter and height control inside for a conventional block. Its drone addendum admits that no empirical research gives an absolute rule for the number of GCPs. The 30-point minimum applies to any project up to 1,000 km². Beyond the 7-coordinate datum minimum, extra GCPs buy redundancy, the ability to detect one wrong point.

**Evidence for the RTK rule**: Przybilla et al. flew RTK drones over test fields. Camera positions alone left height errors of up to 30 ground sample distances, 0.60 m at this resolution; adding one central GCP reduced the systematic height deviations to about 1.5–3 cm, and RTK plus four corner GCPs gave errors within the ground sample distance. Four corner GCPs without camera positions left height errors 15 to 30 times the ground sample distance, and four badly distributed GCPs increased horizontal errors. Check that GCPs and camera positions use the same height system: RTK heights are ellipsoidal. Budget numbers are constructed and recomputed by the check script.

**Blocking errors**: Using checkpoints as GCPs. Putting all control on the perimeter of a self-calibrated nadir block. Assuming RTK removes the need for any ground point. Leaving the survey error out of the budget. Placing checkpoints next to GCPs. Mixing ellipsoidal and orthometric heights.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/154-aerotriangulation-georeferencement|Full reference]]; [ASPRS Ed. 2 V2 (2024), §7.9–§7.10, §7.12.4, §7.13–§7.14, Table C.1, Addendum III §F.4, Addendum V §A.5.9 and §A.7.1](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/251007_gt_cnig_quadogeo_251007_2024_asprs_positional_accuracy_standards_edition2_version2.pdf); [Przybilla et al. 2020, abstract, §2.2.2, §3.2.1 and §4](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLIII-B1-2020/485/2020/isprs-archives-XLIII-B1-2020-485-2020.pdf); [James and Robson 2014, Discussion](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1443475/1/esp3609.pdf). Site, budget and layout are project constructions.

## CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-005

Objective: Compare indirect, direct and integrated sensor orientation by what fixes the datum, what absorbs calibration errors and what control each needs. Family: comparison. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-AEROTRIANGULATION-005
Question: An aerial block flown with a survey camera and a high-end GNSS/IMU system can be oriented in three ways: (i) a bundle block adjustment on tie points and ground control, with the exterior orientations as free unknowns; (ii) exterior orientations taken from the GNSS/IMU and held fixed, object points computed by forward intersection; (iii) GNSS/IMU orientations entered as weighted observations in a bundle adjustment with tie points. Compare them on what fixes the datum and the block shape, how errors of the system calibration (boresight, lever arm, timing, principal distance) are absorbed or detected, how much ground control each needs, and the accuracy you would expect relative to one another. When would you choose each?
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) Route (i), indirect orientation: tie points give the shape and GCPs the datum, so the adjustment interpolates between ground points; the estimated orientations absorb remaining systematic errors, optimal for object points but physically wrong. It needs many well-distributed GCPs and gives the reference accuracy. (2) Route (ii), direct georeferencing: the sensors give the datum and every orientation, an extrapolation from flying height with no redundancy, so boresight, lever-arm, timing or focal-length errors pass into object space undetected (large $\sigma_0$, y-parallax). It needs a calibration flight and checkpoints but no GCP in the mission area, and is about 2–3 times less accurate. (3) Route (iii), integrated sensor orientation: tie points correct the measured orientations within their standard deviations, and boresight and self-calibration parameters can be estimated in situ; constant shifts and vertical offsets still need at least one GCP. Its accuracy approaches route (i) with far less control. Choice: (i) without a trusted GNSS/IMU or for maximum reliability; (iii) for large blocks and corridors with a GCP or two and checkpoints; (ii) for orthophotos, rapid response or inaccessible areas when the tolerance allows.

**Further explanation**: On the OEEPE block Cali10 at 1:10,000, Cramer and Stallmann report checkpoint RMS in X/Y/Z of 4.2/5.3/9.0 cm for GNSS-supported adjustment with self-calibration and 4 GCPs, 16.6/18.6/23.2 cm for fixed GNSS/IMU orientations, and 5.5/7.3/6.0 cm for integrated orientation with self-calibration and 1 GCP, a combined horizontal RMSE of 6.8, 24.9 and 9.1 cm (recomputed by the check script). In phase I of the OEEPE test, direct georeferencing at 1:5,000 reached about 5–10 cm in planimetry and 10–15 cm in height, 2–3 times the standard photogrammetric results; stereo plotting was not yet possible because of y-parallax.

**Why the routes differ**: Cramer et al. showed that self-calibration variants moved estimated orientations by 10 cm to 10 m while object points barely changed, which is why adjusted orientations are poor stand-ins for physical ones. Heipke et al. note that a principal-point shift mimics a timing error and a focal-length change mimics a GNSS height shift, so integrated adjustment needs suitable geometry, such as two flying heights, to separate them. Drones usually carry navigation-grade IMUs, so their integrated variant uses camera positions only and estimates angles by adjustment.

**Blocking errors**: Treating direct georeferencing as integrated orientation with fewer points; it removes the redundancy. Claiming integrated orientation needs no ground information. Using adjusted orientations as physical truth for system calibration. Ranking the routes by GCP count alone.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/154-aerotriangulation-georeferencement|Full reference]]; [Cramer and Stallmann 2001, §4.2–§4.4 and Table 1](https://ifpwww.ifp.uni-stuttgart.de/publications/2001/Cramer_Oeepe2001.pdf); [Heipke et al. 2001, §1, §4 and §5](https://phowo.ifp.uni-stuttgart.de/publications/phowo01/Heipke.pdf); [Cramer et al. 2000, §2.1, Table 2](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIII/congress/part3/198_XXXIII-part3.pdf); [ASPRS Ed. 2 V2 (2024), Addendum III §G and Addendum V §A.4.4](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/251007_gt_cnig_quadogeo_251007_2024_asprs_positional_accuracy_standards_edition2_version2.pdf). Horizontal combination of published RMS values and the choice rules: project derivations.
