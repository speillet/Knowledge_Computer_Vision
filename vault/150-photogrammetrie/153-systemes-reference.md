## CV-GEODESIE-001

Objective: Convert between ellipsoidal heights and national altitudes and predict what mixing them does to an adjustment. Family: understanding. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-GEODESIE-001
Question: A drone survey in mainland France has PPK camera centres in RGF93 v2b geographic 3D coordinates (latitude, longitude, ellipsoidal height). The surveyor delivers the ground control points in Lambert-93 with NGF-IGN69 altitudes. At the site the RAF20 grid value is 49.71 m. One GCP has altitude 195.61 m; the drone flies 120 m above the ground. (a) Which height must this GCP carry to be consistent with the camera centres, and what do the three height quantities mean? (b) If both sets enter a self-calibrating bundle adjustment unchanged, where can the inconsistency go, and with what order of magnitude? (c) Which rule prevents it?
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) $h=H+\zeta=195.61+49.71=245.32$ m. $h$ is a geometric height along the GRS80 ellipsoid normal, the one GNSS delivers; $H$ is a gravity-related altitude above the NGF-IGN69 datum, realized by levelling; $\zeta$ is the separation between the two reference surfaces at that place, valid for RGF93 v2b only, and $H=h-\zeta$. (2) Unchanged, the GCPs sit 49.71 m too low with respect to the cameras: the apparent flying height becomes 169.71 m instead of 120 m, a ratio of 1.414. The adjustment must absorb this somewhere: about 50 m of GNSS position residuals, large GCP reprojection residuals, or a focal length inflated by about 41 % if self-calibration is free, depending on the weights. It is a bias of tens of metres, not noise. (3) Convert every height to one reference with the grid that belongs to the geodetic realization before adjusting; adjust in ellipsoidal heights in a Cartesian frame; convert products to altitudes at the end and record the vertical CRS (NGF-IGN69 height, EPSG:5720) and the grid.

**Further explanation**: The separation is large and varies smoothly: RAF20 values span 42.1 to 56.4 m over its grid of mainland France, and IOGP quotes differences between the WGS 84 ellipsoid and mean sea level from about −100 m to +80 m worldwide. RAF20 is a height-correction surface, not a pure geoid: its predecessor RAF18b adapted the gravimetric quasi-geoid QGF16 to 10,658 levelled GNSS points in RGF93 v2b and is centimetric at one sigma. IOGP applies geoid models and such correction models with the same formula.

**Where the mix enters unnoticed**: Exif 2.32 defines `GPSAltitude` relative to sea level, while COLMAP 4.2.1 stores the EXIF latitude, longitude and altitude as a WGS84 prior and its `EllipsoidToECEF` treats the third value as an ellipsoidal height. GDAL assumes that a height attached to a geographic or projected CRS is ellipsoidal; a gravity-related height needs a compound CRS. PROJ's `vgridshift` subtracts the grid value by default (multiplier −1); with RAF20 it gave $\zeta=43.79$ m at Paris in an executed check.

**Discriminating check**: a vertical offset that is constant over the block and equals the grid value at the site.

**Blocking errors**: Subtracting the grid value instead of adding it, which gives 145.90 m. Treating the 50 m as noise that a robust loss or more GCPs will remove. Assuming that an EXIF or "WGS 84" altitude is ellipsoidal. Using one constant separation for all of France, or a grid with another realization than the one it was computed for.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/153-systemes-reference|Full reference]]; [IOGP 7-2 (2019), §4.1.1 note and §4.11.1: H = h − ζ, geoid versus height-correction models](https://www.iogp.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/373-07-02.pdf); [IGN GEOPOS 2026, pp. 13 and 21: N = height − altitude, RAF20 tied to RGF93 v2b](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/ign_2026-03-26_geopos_refgeodesique_sig_v2.pdf); [IGN GEOPOS 2021, pp. 5 and 7: RAF18b from QGF16](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/documents_wordpress/2021/04/QGF16-et-RAF18b-GEOPOS_2021-03-25.pdf); [PROJ 9.9.0 EPSG data, grid_transformation.sql L1180: RAF20, EPSG 9876](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/data/sql/grid_transformation.sql#L1180); [CIPA Exif 2.32, §4.6.6](https://www.cipa.jp/std/documents/e/DC-X008-Translation-2019-E.pdf); [COLMAP 4.2.1, gps.cc L118–L141](https://github.com/colmap/colmap/blob/4.2.1/src/colmap/geometry/gps.cc#L118-L141); [GDAL 3.13.3, osr_api_tut.rst L389–L395](https://github.com/OSGeo/gdal/blob/v3.13.3/doc/source/tutorials/osr_api_tut.rst#L389-L395). Site values and the flying-height ratio: constructed example, recomputed in the check script.

## CV-GEODESIE-002

Objective: Apply a geographic-to-projected transformation with the correct coordinate order and detect a swapped order. Family: practice. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-GEODESIE-002
Question: A Python step reads ground control points from a GeoJSON file and converts them with pyproj 3.8: `t = Transformer.from_crs("EPSG:4326", "EPSG:2154")`, then `E, N = t.transform(x, y)`, where `x, y` are the first two numbers of each GeoJSON position. For a GCP at 48.8566° N, 2.3522° E it returns E = 6,960,803 m, N = 3,114,983 m, whereas Lambert-93 coordinates in Paris are about (652,469, 6,862,035). A similarity alignment of the SfM model to the converted points then leaves residuals of hundreds of metres. (a) Explain the cause from the conventions of each component. (b) Name two further symptoms that confirm it. (c) Give the fix and a guard that would have caught it.
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) GeoJSON positions are longitude then latitude. EPSG:4326 is defined by its authority with latitude first, and PROJ since version 6, GDAL since 3.0 and pyproj honour that order; `from_crs` defaults to `always_xy=False`. The step therefore read 2.3522 as a latitude and 48.8566 as a longitude, a point off the Horn of Africa, projected more than 7,000 km from Paris. (2) Every point is displaced by a similar huge amount; the layout is mirrored, because the swap has a negative Jacobian determinant, and stretched by about $1/\cos48.86°\approx1.5$ along one direction, so no rotation, scale and translation can fit it: for four GCPs on a 1 km square the best similarity leaves 1,151 m RMS, and even a mirrored fit leaves 233 m. Outputs also fall outside the area of use of Lambert-93. (3) Pass `always_xy=True`, or reorder explicitly to latitude, longitude, or declare the source as OGC:CRS84; in GDAL, set `OAMS_TRADITIONAL_GIS_ORDER`. Guard with a unit test on a known point and its round trip, a check of the CRS axis order (`CRS.axis_info` or `projinfo`) and a bounds check on the outputs.

**Further explanation**: The conflict is between two legitimate conventions. ISO 19111 and the EPSG registry let the authority fix axis order, and EPSG:4326 lists geodetic latitude, then longitude. Most GIS formats, including GeoJSON, write longitude first. Before PROJ 6 and GDAL 3.0 the libraries silently used longitude first, so code that "always worked" breaks after an upgrade. GDAL keeps a data-axis-to-CRS-axis mapping and sets the traditional order on rasters it returns, which is why raster code and vector code can disagree in one program. Projected CRSs can also be northing first.

**Why the swap is not a datum problem**: datum and epoch errors in Europe are at most metres and leave the shape of the block intact; a swap moves points by thousands of kilometres and mirrors the block. The magnitude names the class of error.

**Executed check**: with pyproj 3.8.0 and PROJ 9.8.1, the authority order returns (652,469.02, 6,862,035.26), the swapped pair (6,960,803.48, 3,114,983.26), and `always_xy=True` with longitude first returns the correct value.

**Blocking errors**: Swapping the two output values, which leaves them wrong. Treating the GCPs as outliers for RANSAC or a robust loss. Assuming EPSG:4326 means longitude, latitude because web maps write it that way. Blaming a datum shift.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/153-systemes-reference|Full reference]]; [RFC 7946, §3.1.1: longitude and latitude, in that order](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7946.txt); [PROJ 9.9.0, faq.rst L110–L170: authority axis order](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/docs/source/faq.rst#L110-L170); [GDAL 3.13.3, osr_api_tut.rst L176–L228](https://github.com/OSGeo/gdal/blob/v3.13.3/doc/source/tutorials/osr_api_tut.rst#L176-L228) and [RFC 73, L259–L372](https://github.com/OSGeo/gdal/blob/v3.13.3/doc/source/development/rfc/rfc73_proj6_wkt2_srsbarn.rst#L259-L372); [pyproj 3.8.0, transformer.py L668–L733: always_xy](https://github.com/pyproj4/pyproj/blob/3.8.0/pyproj/transformer.py#L668-L733); [PROJ 9.9.0 EPSG data, axis.sql L265–L266](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/data/sql/axis.sql#L265-L266). Similarity residuals and Jacobian: constructed example, recomputed in the check script.

## CV-GEODESIE-003

Objective: Explain, with orders of magnitude, why a block should not be adjusted in projected planimetry with altitudes. Family: reasoning. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-GEODESIE-003
Question: An aerial block 10 km wide in central France, near 46.5° N where the Lambert-93 point scale factor is 0.99905, is adjusted with its GCPs and GNSS camera centres given as Lambert-93 easting and northing plus NGF-IGN69 altitude, and the software treats these three numbers as a Cartesian frame. Take an Earth radius of 6,380 km and a calibrated camera with f = 4,000 px. (a) What is wrong with this object space? Quantify both geometric effects, at 1, 2, 5 and 10 km from the block centre and per kilometre of horizontal distance. (b) How can these effects appear in the adjustment results? (c) What should be done instead?
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) Easting–northing–altitude is not Euclidean. The projection is conformal but not isometric: map lengths are the ellipsoidal ones times $k$, here 0.99905, so 0.95 m shorter per kilometre, while altitudes stay true metres; $k$ also varies across France, equal to 1 on 44° N and 49° N and above 1.002 in the far north. Altitudes are measured from a curved reference, so a level surface looks flat: a point at distance $d$ is $d^2/(2R)$ below the tangent plane, 0.08, 0.31, 1.96 and 7.84 m at 1, 2, 5 and 10 km. (2) The horizontal-only scale error conflicts with the calibrated focal length: a free focal length absorbs $f/k-f\approx3.8$ px; with $f$ fixed, about 1.9 px of radial misfit remains 2,000 px from the image centre, or the camera heights shift by about 1 m per kilometre of flying height. Curvature is absorbed as camera attitudes tilted by $d/R$, 0.31 mrad at 2 km, which conflicts with INS attitudes, or as height residuals growing with $d^2$, and in wide frame images as a radial pattern that distortion terms can absorb. (3) Adjust in a local East–North–Up frame at the block centre, or in ECEF, with ellipsoidal heights; convert inputs in and products out with recorded operations.

**Further explanation**: Collinearity assumes straight rays in a Euclidean space, and Heipke et al. note that "most mapping systems do not fulfil" this requirement. In the OEEPE test, one GNSS/IMU data set gave significantly better planimetry and especially height in a local tangential system than in UTM, the other showed no difference; the authors recommend the tangential system, or calibrating in the imposed mapping system. IGN's MicMac documentation builds a local tangent system for the same reason.

**Further orders of magnitude**: grid north departs from true north by the convergence $n(\lambda-3°)$ with $n=0.7256$, −5.4° at Brest, so kappa angles differ from INS headings. Terrain at 1,000 m adds 157 ppm between terrain and ellipsoid distances. For a frame camera with f = 100 mm at 3,000 m, a point 2 km from nadir appears 7 µm too close to the centre. These figures are constructed project derivations; the size of the effect in a given program depends on corrections it may apply internally.

**Blocking errors**: Calling map coordinates true metres because their unit is the metre. Assuming the effect is negligible because tie-point residuals are small. Correcting only the vertical datum. Letting self-calibration absorb the scale and reporting the focal length as a camera property.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/153-systemes-reference|Full reference]]; [Heipke, Jacobsen and Wegmann 2001, §1 and §4](https://phowo.ifp.uni-stuttgart.de/publications/phowo01/Heipke.pdf); [Snyder 1987, pp. 21, 105 and 108, eqs. 14-16, 15-6 to 15-10: convergence and LCC scale factor](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/1395/report.pdf); [IOGP 7-2 (2019), §3.1.1.1](https://www.iogp.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/373-07-02.pdf); [PROJ 9.9.0 EPSG data, conversion.sql L4881: Lambert-93 parameters](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/data/sql/conversion.sql#L4881); [MicMac documentation a647a96, QuickStartSimplified-Tools-2.tex L524–L531](https://github.com/micmacIGN/Documentation/blob/a647a96b177c2ea047917a22b59aa133464fbc6a/DocMicMac/Generalites/QuickStartSimplified-Tools-2.tex#L524-L531). Curvature drops, focal absorption and radial pattern: project derivations, recomputed in the check script.

## CV-GEODESIE-004

Objective: Compute local East–North–Up coordinates from geodetic coordinates through the geocentric frame. Family: algorithm. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-GEODESIE-004
Question: A drone block will be adjusted in a local Cartesian frame. Its origin is O = (48.000° N, 2.000° E, ellipsoidal height 150 m) on GRS80, with a = 6,378,137 m and 1/f = 298.257222101. A checkpoint is at (48.010° N, 2.010° E, 160 m). Give the algorithm, with every formula, that maps geodetic coordinates to East–North–Up coordinates at O; estimate the result for this checkpoint (horizontal components to a few metres, Up to the centimetre); explain why the Up coordinate is not 10 m and what the Up axis points to.
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) Convert both points to geocentric coordinates, angles in radians: $e^2=2f-f^2$, $\nu=a/\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}$, $X=(\nu+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda$, $Y=(\nu+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda$, $Z=((1-e^2)\nu+h)\sin\varphi$. (2) Subtract the origin and rotate: $[E,N,U]^\top=R\,(P-O)$ with rows $(-\sin\lambda_0,\cos\lambda_0,0)$, $(-\sin\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0,-\sin\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0,\cos\varphi_0)$ and $(\cos\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0,\cos\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0,\sin\varphi_0)$; $R$ is a proper rotation and its inverse is $R^\top$. (3) Result: E ≈ 746 m and N ≈ 1,112 m, since 0.01° of latitude is about 1.112 km and 0.01° of longitude about 1.113 km × cos 48°; the exact values are 746.13 m and 1,111.98 m. U = 9.86 m: Up is 0.14 m below the 10 m height difference because the tangent plane at O rises above the ellipsoid by $d^2/(2R)$, with $d=1{,}339$ m and $R=\sqrt{\rho\nu}\approx6{,}380$ km. The Up axis is the ellipsoid normal at O, not the plumb line, and the frame is right-handed and metric.

**Further explanation**: At 48°, $\nu=6{,}389{,}960$ m and the meridian radius $\rho=6{,}370{,}736$ m; O lies at (4,273,213.461, 149,223.902, 4,716,987.802) m in ECEF. The same chain is PROJ's `cart` then `topocentric` steps, EPSG method 9837, and COLMAP's `GPSTransform` uses the same matrix. Validate an implementation against an authority example before trusting it: IOGP's worked case gives U = −189,013.869 m, V = −128,642.040 m, W = −4,220.171 m, and the check script reproduces it to the millimetre.

**Implementation details that bite**: degrees must become radians; the latitude is geodetic, not geocentric; the height must be ellipsoidal, not an altitude. Subtract the origin in double precision: ECEF coordinates near 4.27 million metres are only representable every 0.5 m in float32. A NED frame is $(N,E,-U)$; a gravity-aligned VIO world frame is close to ENU but not identical. Another origin gives another rotation, so the origin belongs to the frame definition.

**Blocking errors**: Computing ENU by scaling degree differences by constant metres per degree, or flattening latitude and longitude into a plane. Applying the rotation to absolute ECEF coordinates instead of their difference. Using $R^\top$ for the forward direction. Calling Up the gravity direction.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/153-systemes-reference|Full reference]]; [IOGP 7-2 (2019), §1.1, §4.1.1 and §4.1.2 with worked examples](https://www.iogp.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/373-07-02.pdf); [PROJ 9.9.0, topocentric.rst L21–L71](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/docs/source/operations/conversions/topocentric.rst#L21-L71); [PROJ 9.9.0, cart.rst L21–L33](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/docs/source/operations/conversions/cart.rst#L21-L33); [COLMAP 4.2.1, gps.cc L191–L217](https://github.com/colmap/colmap/blob/4.2.1/src/colmap/geometry/gps.cc#L191-L217). Checkpoint values: constructed example, recomputed in the check script and by an executed pyproj pipeline.

## CV-GEODESIE-005

Objective: Diagnose a frame and epoch mismatch between global GNSS control and a European national frame. Family: diagnosis. Provenance: agent.

START
CV-Pilote
ID: CV-GEODESIE-005
Question: A drone block at 48.0° N, 2.0° E is georeferenced on GNSS control points processed by precise point positioning with IGS20 products, that is in ITRF2020 at the observation epoch 2026.87. Independent checkpoints from the national network are given in RGF93 v2b, the French realization of ETRS89, published as ETRF2000 coordinates for epoch 2019.0. The checkpoint residuals show a nearly constant horizontal offset, a few centimetres vertically, and good relative accuracy. In ITRF2020 the site moves 1.80 cm/yr east and 1.59 cm/yr north. (a) Estimate the size and direction of the offset. (b) Explain it and give the discriminating check. (c) Give the fix and the metadata rule.
Reponse: **Expected answer**: (1) About 0.9 m, the product lying to the north-east of the checkpoints. ETRS89 coincided with the ITRS at epoch 1989.0 and is fixed to the stable Eurasian plate, so the gap grows with plate motion since 1989.0, not since 2019.0: $2.40\ \text{cm/yr}\times37.87\ \text{yr}\approx0.91$ m. With the frame offsets included, the 14-parameter transformation ITRF2020 to ETRF2000 evaluated at 2026.87 gives ITRF2020 coordinates 0.66 m east and 0.68 m north of the national ones, 0.945 m at azimuth 44°, and 1.7 cm higher. (2) The pattern is a translation: same size and direction over the block, horizontal much larger than vertical, no scale or rotation signature, while tie points and relative accuracy are fine. The check is to evaluate the transformation at the observation epoch: the residuals collapse to their noise. A typical cause is a "WGS 84 = ETRS89" null transformation, which EPSG rates at 1 m. (3) Transform the control from ITRF2020 at its epoch to RGF93 v2b before the adjustment, with EPSG 10586 at 2026.87, or process the GNSS data directly in the national frame; store with every coordinate the CRS, its realization and the coordinate epoch.

**Further explanation**: ITRF2020 is a dynamic frame: a point on stable Europe has a velocity of a few centimetres per year in it. ETRS89 realizations remove the Eurasian rotation, so national coordinates of stable points barely change. EUREF writes the relation as $X^E(t)=X^I(t)+T+\dot{\mathcal R}X^I(t)(t-1989.0)$ and gives the 14 parameters of ITRF2020 to ETRF2000 at 2015.0 with their rates. IGN summarizes it: at about 2.6 cm/yr the ITRS–ETRS89 gap is nearly 1 m in 2026. WGS 84 (G2296), implemented in NGA's precise orbit and clock products since 7 January 2024, is aligned to ITRF2020: coordinates in a recent WGS 84 realization sit close to ITRF2020, not to ETRS89.

**Why the elapsed time matters**: the 2019.0 date is the epoch at which RGF93 v2b coordinates were refreshed, not the date at which ETRS89 left the ITRS. The rotation about $z$ alone, −29.99 mas at 2026.87, moves this point by 0.62 m.

**Blocking errors**: Multiplying the velocity by 2026.87 − 2019.0 and expecting 0.19 m. Treating WGS 84, ITRF2020 and ETRS89 as interchangeable. Fitting a translation on the checkpoints, which then stop being independent and hide the rule. Looking for a vertical-datum or antenna-height error.
Source: [[150-photogrammetrie/references/153-systemes-reference|Full reference]]; [EUREF TN1 (Altamimi and Collilieux 2024), §2 eqs. 2–3, Table 4 and Appendix B](https://etrs89.ign.fr/pub/EUREF-TN-1-Mar-04-2024.pdf); [IGN GEOPOS 2026, pp. 5–6: realizations of RGF93, ITRS–ETRS89 gap](https://cnig.gouv.fr/IMG/pdf/ign_2026-03-26_geopos_refgeodesique_sig_v2.pdf); [PROJ 9.9.0 EPSG data, helmert_transformation.sql L2598 and L2785: null WGS 84 shift and EPSG 10586](https://github.com/OSGeo/PROJ/blob/9.9.0/data/sql/helmert_transformation.sql#L2598); [NGA, IGSMAIL-8444 (2024): WGS 84 (G2296) aligned to ITRF2020](https://lists.igs.org/pipermail/igsmail/2024/008440.html); [IOGP 7-2 (2019), §4.2.5: time-dependent Helmert](https://www.iogp.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/373-07-02.pdf). Site, epoch and offsets: constructed example, recomputed in the check script, which first reproduces EUREF TN1 Appendix B.
